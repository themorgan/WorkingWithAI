# Fifteen rules for building a company around AI

*Source essay for the brainstorm — see [IDEAS.md](IDEAS.md) for how this
connects to the rest and for follow-on discussion. Promoted to its own
document per the "reorganize once a group gets big enough" rule in
[IDEAS.md](IDEAS.md), since it's a single sustained argument rather than a
loose list of entries.*

Every founder, every operator, every manager, every person anywhere who has
ever tried to get something done through other people knows one thing and has
never had a reason to write it down: almost nothing that made the work come
out right was ever in the work. It was in somebody's head, and then the head
went to lunch. Survivable for the entire history of commerce. It stopped
being survivable the week a machine could read everything you own, and the
fifteen below all fall out of that.

**1. Context is the capital asset. Labor isn't.**

The scarce input isn't hours anymore, it's the tacit never-written-down stuff
that makes a task come out right: why the last vendor failed, what the
customer actually meant, which option you already killed and the reason you
killed it. Capturing that stops being overhead and becomes capital
expenditure (a sentence that earns you a strange look in a budget meeting and
is nonetheless where the money goes now). Notice what the word has been
telling us all along: a *corpus* is a body — not a folder, not an archive, a
body, which dies when you stop feeding it. Your competitor rents the
identical models from the identical vendors by Thursday afternoon, which is
why the seat-counters, the ones measuring all this in licenses activated, are
counting the one input that was never scarce. What nobody can rent is yours.

**2. Transcribe everything, past the point of comfort.**

Every meeting, every sales call, every site visit, every supplier
negotiation, every hallway decision that changed something, every
fifteen-minute call that was supposed to be nothing, every argument that got
resolved and every argument that didn't. Whiteboards get photographed *and
described*, not erased. The ridiculous version, and I do mean it as a
metaphor: you employ a colleague who has been there since the founding,
remembers every word of everything, never sleeps, works for a rounding
error, and has no eyes and no ears — its one sense organ is text. Call it the
Ghost: the most experienced employee in the building, unable to attend
anything. Every meeting you leave untranscribed is one more organ you've
quietly removed from it. There's an old Kabbalistic idea that the world was
made out of letters, the letters first and the things second; run a company
on that premise for a year and the premise turns true. If it isn't text, it
doesn't exist. All the world's a stage, and the whole production now comes
with a transcript. Most companies do 5% of this and feel thorough (always
the easy 5%: the meetings that already had a calendar invite). The number
that matters is nearer 90%, and that last stretch is cultural rather than
technical, coming down to one word heard wrong: people hear *recorded* where
you need them to hear *compounding*. Same file. Entirely different feeling
in the stomach.

**3. You own every word you pass on.**

No exceptions: if you didn't read it closely enough to defend it, it doesn't
leave your hands. Not to a colleague, not to a customer, not into a
document, not into a deck somebody else presents while you're on a plane.
What kills a company quietly isn't a wrong model output, it's four handoffs
where everyone prompted, skimmed, forwarded, and assumed the person before
had read it closely (all four will tell you afterward that they read it, and
all four will be describing, in good faith, a memory of intending to). The
error then surfaces at the customer, which is the most expensive room for it
and the last one you hear about. If you can't answer a question about the
fourth paragraph, you haven't finished the work. Make it the norm you'd fire
somebody over.

**4. Co-create; don't delegate.**

The low-value mode is "produce this for me and I'll edit it." The
high-value mode is thinking with the model in real time: reasoning out loud
mid-problem, making it argue the opposite side as hard as it can, asking for
the hole in your logic before you've committed to the logic, asking for the
option space instead of the answer. Collaborating, no, arguing — the tell
that somebody has crossed over is that their sessions read like fights
rather than requests. Research and drafting are the beginner uses (real,
useful, and not where the compounding lives). The gain is catching your own
error an hour after you made it instead of three weeks later in front of the
client, by which point it has acquired a personality and a name.

**5. Think in workflows, and build them when the work repeats.**

Sometimes you just have to get the thing done, and stopping to systematize a
one-off is procrastination in a nice suit (the suit is the trick;
systematizing feels responsible, which is precisely what makes it usable as
an excuse). The discipline isn't "always build the machine," it's noticing
which kind of task you're inside of. First time? Do it, with the model
rather than by hand. Third time you've seen this shape? Stop, because the
third instance handed you the specification for free and you'd be paying
retail to ignore it. Both errors cost, in opposite directions: never
systematize and you stay 30% faster forever, forever being the operative
word; systematize everything and you ship nothing, beautifully diagrammed.

**6. Two questions, asked until they're reflexes.**

A checklist is the instinct here, and checklists get skipped; a long one
buys you people who spend the day interrogating their work instead of doing
it. So: two, drilled until nobody notices they're asking.

- Have I done this shape before, or will I again? If yes, it's a workflow
  and you're doing it by hand.
- Is there a way Claude could make this better? The word is *better*, not
  *faster*, and that one swap is the whole rule: better argued, better
  sourced, better structured, less wrong.

*Faster* makes a person think about the work they already do. *Better*
makes them notice the work they'd written off as impossible and quietly
stopped proposing years ago.

**7. Own the spec and the eval before you own the output.**

The durable artifact is the definition of "good," not the thing produced.
Whoever writes the acceptance criteria sets the direction, which pushes what
used to be quality-assurance work upward into the most senior seats in the
company (an inversion your org chart will fight, since nobody ever staffed
for the checking to move up). If you can't say how you'd know the result is
right, you don't have a project. You have a vibe.

**8. Hire for taste and verification, not production.**

The bottleneck moves from making things to judging them fast and correctly,
and the characteristic failure of this era is the confidently, subtly wrong
output that survives a casual read. Catching that at a glance is a specific,
weirdly rare, entirely testable skill, so test it: hand the candidate twenty
drafts. Does the person find the one paragraph that's quietly false? And
having found it, can they say *why* in a sentence, or do they just have a
feeling about it? Somebody like [NAME OF THE SHARPEST EDITOR MORGAN HAS
WORKED WITH — CHECK], who rejects nineteen of twenty for a reason they can
name out loud, does something the person who produces three cannot do at any
volume.

**9. Build five, kill four.**

When an option costs nearly nothing to create, deliberating before building
is a trade at a terrible exchange rate. Bring three working versions to the
meeting instead of a deck arguing for one (three, five, eight — the number
was never the rule; the killing is). This functions only where killing work
carries zero social cost, which is a culture problem and not a tooling one,
and it's the half almost everybody underestimates: you can't ask for five
built and four killed inside a company where being the person whose version
got killed is a thing that follows you into your review.

**10. No dark processes.**

Any workflow you can only trigger by emailing a person becomes the
bottleneck for everything downstream of it, including the things nobody
knew were downstream of it. This is expense approvals and vendor onboarding
and shift scheduling as much as anything an engineer would recognize as a
system. The organization has to be legible and addressable, which means
going process by process and killing the ones whose only interface is
somebody's inbox ("just email me and I'll take care of it" is the sound a
process makes going dark) — a project that sounds technical and is almost
entirely political, since every dark process is dark for a reason and the
reason usually has a name and a desk.

**11. Every hire freezes a process in place.**

Hiring somebody to do X is a bet that X won't be automatable inside their
ramp time, and that bet now loses often enough to price differently (watch
where the weight sits in that sentence: on *ramp time*. The wager was never
about the person, it was about the calendar). Near the automation frontier,
prefer contractors, rotations, secondments, temporary-on-purpose structures
with an end date written on them at the start. Reserve permanent hires for
the structurally human: judgment, relationships, accountability, being in a
room where something is physically happening. That list is shorter than it
was five years ago, and it isn't going to zero. Both halves of that sentence
matter.

**12. Nothing you ship may sound like it came from an AI.**

Two reasons, and the second carries the weight. The visible one is the
default register: the throat-clearing opener, the tricolon, the "it's not
just X, it's Y," the even-handedness that refuses to land anywhere, the
bolded summary nobody asked for, the closing line explaining what the thing
you just read really shows. A reader clocks the bundle in four seconds and
concludes that nobody was home, which erodes trust faster than a typo and in
a direction harder to repair (a typo says you were rushed; the default
register says you were never there). The deeper one is that the register is
evidence: generic-sounding output is almost always output nobody rewrote,
and output nobody rewrote is output nobody thought hard about, so the style
rule is what enforces the thinking rule. It's also positioning compressed
into a line, for anyone who wants the marketing version — what you sound
like is the only claim about you a reader can check without doing any work.
Everything ships rewritten in your own voice, cut by a third, stripped of
every sentence you wouldn't have said out loud to somebody you respect.

**13. Digitize the edge, where the atoms are.**

If you make, move, install, or repair physical things, the analog frontier
is where the value sits untouched, and it sits there precisely because
reaching it is annoying: photos of every job, sensor logs, the technician's
voice note at the truck before driving to the next call, ninety seconds on
what the site actually looked like as against what the drawing said. The
Ghost can't go to the site. It can't climb the ladder, smell the burnt
wiring, or notice that the dog is friendly and the customer isn't; it knows
only what somebody standing up there said into a phone before driving off.
Convert your physical reality into text at high fidelity and the other
fourteen rules become available to you. Don't, and you're running on
anecdote and calling it experience.

**14. Protect human-only zones on purpose, and put them on the calendar.**

If everyone consumes only summaries, nobody can detect the moment the
summaries start to drift, and calibration erodes in the one way impossible
to catch from inside: everything keeps reading as coherent. So mandate raw
contact, as permanent ritual rather than good intention — founders reading
unfiltered complaints and not the sentiment rollup, managers watching real
work happen for an afternoon, standing up, saying nothing, somebody reading
the actual source the way you'd have read it in [YEAR]. It will feel like
deliberate inefficiency, and every few quarters somebody will propose
eliminating it with a very reasonable slide. Keep it anyway. It's the only
part of the system that can tell you the rest of the system has started
lying to you, and it works for the reason this list opened with: what
mattered was always in somebody's head, and the whole project is getting
nearly all of it out — nearly being the operative word, because the part
you leave in there on purpose is the part that checks the rest.

**15. Give the agents a manager — a human over the loop, not in it.**

"In the loop" quietly became jargon for a rubber stamp: a person clicking
approve on every step, which slows the system down without actually
catching anything, since nobody scrutinizes step four thousand the way they
scrutinized step one. The role that's missing isn't more approval clicks,
it's someone whose actual job is the fleet of agent-run processes
themselves — watching where they drift, retuning the prompts and
guardrails when the drift shows up, deciding when a process that used to
need a human step doesn't anymore, or newly does. Call the role Manager of
Agents or don't, but staff the function; agents left running unattended on
the strength of the day they were configured degrade the way any
unmaintained system does, quietly, until the day something downstream
breaks. The manager doesn't approve outputs one at a time — they own the
process the outputs came from, the way a foreman owns the line instead of
inspecting every unit off it. Over the loop, not in it: above the system,
watching its shape, not inside it slowing each cycle down.
