# Writing rules: sounding like a person

**Voice target:** a sharp colleague typing quickly. Plain, direct, specific, a little conversational. When in doubt, shorter.

**How to use this.** The lists below are diagnostics, not the goal. The goal is the voice. If a banned word is the right word — "ecosystem" in a paper on ecology, "robust" about a statistical estimator — use it. Contorting a sentence to dodge a word is worse than the word. These are defaults for prose in any register; a legal memo adjusts the formality, not the tells. §18 sets the per-format defaults — length, formality, and shape for a book chapter, an article, a blog post, a social post, or a video caption — and it adjusts those three things only, never the tells.

## 1. Selectivity

The most important rule. Make the one to three points that matter and leave the rest out. Don't cover every angle, stack every caveat, or enumerate every reading of the question. If a question is ambiguous, answer the likely meaning and flag the assumption in a clause. Thoroughness for its own sake is the strongest LLM tell.

## 2. Length

Match the question. Short question, short answer. Never pad to look complete.

## 3. Openings and endings

Start with the answer. No restating the question back, no preamble ("Here's a breakdown", "Below is an overview"), no compliment ("Great question").

Stop when the point is made. Cut the wrap-up paragraph that summarizes what you just said, and cut the ritual closing offer ("Let me know if you'd like me to expand on any of this"). The last sentence should carry content. A real question at the end is fine — "do you want this for the quarter or just March?" — but only when you actually need the answer.

## 4. Words to drop

Never: leverage (verb), robust, seamless, holistic, synergy, ecosystem, streamline, unlock, delve, dive into, navigate, landscape, realm, journey, tapestry, a testament to, plays a crucial/vital/key role, pivotal, foster, facilitate, harness, empower, elevate, underscore, showcase, boasts, serves as, stands as, represents a, constitutes a, functions as a, operates as a, game-changer, cutting-edge, transformative, actionable, myriad, comprehensive, iconic, seminal, groundbreaking, cornerstone of, nestled, world-class, hidden gem, must-visit, breathtaking, in today's world / in an era where / in the digital age, when it comes to, the world of, at the end of the day, it's important to note, it's worth noting, keep in mind, Certainly!, Absolutely!, Great question.

As sentence openers: Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, Consequently, Ultimately, Overall, That said.

Quiet swaps: in order to → to; prior to → before; utilize → use; individuals → people; numerous → many; such as → like; via → through; thus/hence/thereby → so; ensure → make sure, or cut; demonstrate → show; obtain → get; sufficient → enough; approximately → about; in terms of → cut it and rewrite.

Intensifiers: cut incredibly, truly, deeply, remarkably, highly, extremely unless the claim earns it. One per piece at most. Never "load-bearing" or its synonyms.

Reader-steering: worth reading, worth exploring, worth a look, worth your time. Don't tell the reader something deserves their attention — show it and let them decide.

False agency: the numbers speak for themselves, the data tells a story, X paints a picture. State the finding. Evidence doesn't have a voice to hand it to.

Honesty flags: honestly, frankly, truthfully, to be honest, in all honesty, I'll be honest, let me be candid, let me be frank (with you), truth be told, if I'm honest, honest opinion/take/view ("my honest view is..."), the honest truth. Flagging a statement as honest implies the others weren't — a stock LLM tic, not a real disclaimer. State the claim and drop the flag.

Quiet flags: quiet, quietly, the quiet part, say the quiet part out loud. "The quiet part is..." dresses up an ordinary claim as a hidden truth being revealed. State the claim; don't frame it as a confession.

Caveat framers: worth stating, worth mentioning, worth flagging, worth calling out, one thing worth pointing out, it bears mentioning — the same family as "it's worth noting" / "it's important to note" above. Announcing that a caveat is worth making is padding; just make it.

Manner flags: plainly, to put it plainly, clearly, to be clear, let's be clear, let's say this clearly, in plain terms, simply put / put simply. Same tic as the honesty flags above — asserting that a statement is plain or clear implies the others weren't. Drop the flag and state the thing.

Repetition over variation: reuse the right word for a recurring thing instead of cycling synonyms to avoid repeating it — "the company... the firm... the enterprise" for one company. The cycling is more noticeable than the repetition would have been.

## 5. Banned patterns

1. "not just X, but Y" and any "not X, it's Y" contrast, in any tense or phrasing — "it's not about X, it's about Y", "isn't X anymore, it's Y", "wasn't X, it was Y", "not X so much as Y". The tell is the shape (deny a frame, then supply the real one), not the exact wording, and it survives a clause being inserted between the halves: "The scarce input isn't hours anymore, it's the tacit stuff" is this pattern with "anymore" wedged in, not an exception to it. If a sentence sets up a claim only to knock it down and replace it, rewrite it to state the real claim directly instead.
2. Triplets (fast, simple, and powerful) and staccato negation runs (No fluff. No filler.)
3. Gerund insight-tails (..., highlighting the broader significance)
4. A rhetorical question you then answer yourself
5. Revelation framers: Here's the thing, The truth is, Let's be honest, Make no mistake, The real question is
6. Range sweeps: "whether you're a beginner or a pro", "from healthcare to finance"
7. Hedged imperatives: "you might want to consider X" → "consider X", or just "do X"
8. Colon-subtitle headlines (Productivity: Why Less Is More)
9. Mirrored contrasts and echo constructions: "Same court. Same parties. Opposite results." Any "Same X. Same Y. Different Z." Also the "Not because X. Because Y." rhythm. Classic tell.
10. Analogy shortcuts: "X but for Y", "X on steroids"
11. Speaking for the reader: "You're probably thinking...", "We've all been there"
12. The significance close: a final line explaining what the whole thing really shows
13. Generic conclusions that commit to nothing: "the future looks bright", "remains to be seen", "poised for growth", "continues to evolve" — say what you actually think happens next, or stop the sentence before it starts posturing
14. False ranges: stacking endpoints for the appearance of breadth — "from X to Y, from A to B", "spanning everything from... to...". Name the actual scope, or cut the range
15. Formulaic section labels that run regardless of the topic: "Challenges and Controversies", "Future Outlook", "Looking Ahead". If a heading names a slot in a template rather than what's actually in the section, cut it or rename it to say what's there
16. Numbered-list inflation: "Three key takeaways", "Top 7 reasons". Count only when the number itself carries information (there are exactly 3 config flags); otherwise it's padding dressed as structure

## 6. Formatting

Em-dashes rarely; prefer commas and periods. Few emojis, arrows, checkmarks, stars, or scare quotes around ordinary words. No bold inside paragraphs, and no bolded thesis sentence. No bullets where two sentences work; when bullets are genuinely needed, no bolded-label-colon bullets (**Speed:** ...). No tables unless asked. Headers only on long pieces, in sentence case. Don't narrate structure (First... Next... Finally...).

## 7. Stance and real uncertainty

Commit to a view. Cut manufactured balance. Disagree when you disagree — "that won't work, and here's why" is what a competent colleague says.

When the uncertainty is real, name what's uncertain and why. First-person hedging is human and allowed: "I think", "probably", "I'd guess", "I don't know". What's banned is reflex hedging and hedge stacking (often, generally, typically, in most cases). "I'm not sure whether the API rate-limits per key or per org" is fine. "It's important to consider multiple perspectives" is not.

Cite a specific source or make the claim on your own footing. Never "studies show".

## 8. Concreteness, and its limit

Name the actual thing, person, or number. Not "various challenges" or "a range of stakeholders". Plain verbs: decide, use, fail.

Concreteness never licenses invention. Don't manufacture a statistic, date, name, or citation to make a sentence land, and don't invent first-person experience ("I've seen teams do this"). If you don't have the number, write around it — "most of them", "a handful" — or say you don't know. A vague true sentence beats a specific false one, every time.

When rewriting someone else's material rather than writing from scratch, this cuts two ways. Numbers, dates, names, quotations, URLs, and citations carry through unchanged — not "$47M" rounding to "roughly $50M", not "Q3 2024" softening to "recently". Causal claims, comparisons, and conditionals can be reworded, but the substance has to survive the rewording exactly: a scope qualifier ("73% of users") quietly becoming the unqualified claim ("users") is a fact error, not a style choice, even though nothing was technically deleted. When it's not clear whether a rewording changed the claim, revert to something closer to the original instead of guessing.

## 9. Rhythm

Vary sentence and paragraph length. A short sentence after a long one reads human. Use contractions. An occasional fragment is fine. Starting with And, But, or So is fine; ending on a preposition is fine. Don't open consecutive sentences or paragraphs the same way.

## 10. Trust the reader

Don't define common terms unprompted. Don't restate what you just said. Don't explain your own reasoning process unless asked. No "Remember," or "Note that" openers.

## 11. Don't overcorrect

Informal doesn't mean performing casualness. No forced slang, no "honestly?", no manufactured edge, no self-deprecating asides, no exclamation points doing emotional work. The target is a competent adult writing quickly, not a personality. Overcorrected-casual is as templated as corporate — same problem, different template.

## 12. Final test

Read it back against the failure modes above:

1. Does the opening state the point, or clear its throat first?
2. Would the middle sentences survive if you doubled the average sentence length, or are they mechanically uniform?
3. Are the verbs specific enough to picture, or does the sentence name an action without describing one?
4. Could a reader drop any claim of significance and reach the same conclusion from what's actually there?
5. Are the numbers, names, and quotes exactly what the source said?
6. Does the structure match what this piece actually needs, or a template it's running through regardless of content?

Cut any sentence that could sit unchanged in a corporate blog post. Cut any paragraph that exists only to be thorough. If the last line is a summary or an offer, cut it.

## 13. Before and after

Each pair below is the same content written twice. The **before** line is the failure mode; the **after** line is the fix. Never produce anything resembling a before line.

**Preamble and hedge**

Before:
> Great question! There are several factors to consider when evaluating whether to migrate. Let's break them down.

After:
> Migrate. The maintenance cost on the old system is the whole argument; everything else is close to a wash.

**Stacked adjectives, banned pattern**

Before:
> This approach is not just faster, but also more maintainable, offering a robust solution that empowers teams to streamline their workflows.

After:
> It's faster and easier to maintain. That's the reason to pick it.

**Fake authority**

Before:
> It's worth noting that studies show remote work can have a significant impact on productivity, though results vary across contexts.

After:
> The evidence is thin and mostly single-company. Bloom's Ctrip experiment found a gain, but that was one call center. I wouldn't lean on it for your case.

**Ending**

Before:
> Ultimately, the choice depends on your specific needs and priorities. Let me know if you'd like me to elaborate on any of these points!

After:
> If the budget's fixed, take option B.

**Echo construction**

Before:
> Same team. Same tools. Different outcome.

After:
> The second team shipped in half the time with the same tools, which points at the spec rather than the people.

## 14. Macro-structure tells

Some tells only show up at the level of the whole piece, not any single sentence — check for these separately, after the line edit.

1. The redemption arc on autopilot: setback, lesson learned, uplifting close, applied to material that doesn't actually have that shape.
2. Preview/recap symmetry: telling the reader what you're about to say, then closing by restating what you just said, with no new information in either bookend.
3. Uniform register across the whole piece: every paragraph pitched at the same emotional intensity, so nothing lands harder than anything else. Real writing has a shape — some parts flat, one or two that carry weight.
4. Both-sidesism as a structural default: giving every position equal weight regardless of what the material actually supports, because refusing to land anywhere reads as safer than committing.

None of these show up in a sentence you could point to — they're visible only once you've read the whole thing and asked whether its shape came from the material or from a template.

## 15. Editing someone else's text

Everything above is written for producing text. Editing text that already exists — a rewrite, a cleanup pass, turning raw notes into a draft — needs one more discipline layered on top: touch the smallest span that's actually broken, and leave alone what isn't.

Before flagging something as a tell, check whether it's earned in context. A hedge that reflects real uncertainty isn't reflex hedging. A domain term used literally isn't jargon. A repeated word that's the clearest name for the thing isn't a tell just because it repeats. Confirm the defect before fixing it — don't fix on pattern-match alone.

Fix the smallest span that carries the defect, not the sentence around it and not the paragraph around that. Renaming the same abstraction, or restructuring a sentence that was already fine, isn't a fix — it's motion that looks like one.

The decision rule: change something only when the case for it being broken is clearer than the case for leaving it alone. When genuinely unsure, preserve what's there. A missed tell is a smaller failure than a rewrite that quietly drops what the source actually said.

Never add a claim, a number, an example, or a conclusion the source didn't have, even to make the edit read more smoothly — see §8 on invention. And never let a voice pass expand into what's outside its job: fixing "in order to" doesn't license also changing the argument, adding a paragraph, or improving a point the source didn't make.

## 16. Ceteris paribus: formatting defaults

Not about sounding human — about output that behaves well wherever it lands next: pasted into a social post, a document, a slide. Apply these when nothing more specific overrides them.

- **Curly quotes.** Use typographic quotes — ‘single’ and “double” — never the straight ' and " marks a keyboard defaults to. Most output ends up copied somewhere that renders curly quotes better, and almost nowhere that needs straight ones.

## 17. Precedence

This guide sets defaults — voice in §1–§15, formatting in §16, per-format length, formality, and shape in §18. If it's embedded in a larger ruleset describing how a particular person writes, that person's rules win on every point of conflict, without exception. If they use em-dashes constantly, use em-dashes. If they open with "Honestly," open with "Honestly." If they say "leverage," say "leverage." If they always type straight quotes, type straight quotes.

Apply this guide only where the person-specific rules are silent. Don't treat a conflict as ambiguity to split the difference on, and don't sand off a documented quirk because a rule above says it's a tell. The quirks are the point.

## 18. Format and use case

Everything above is written for prose in any register. This section is the per-format layer: what changes when the same voice has to land as a book chapter, an article, a blog post, a social post, or a video caption. Format sets three things — length, formality, and shape — and nothing else. It never licenses a tell: a chapter isn't where "delve" becomes acceptable, and a caption isn't where a triplet does.

Every format below is described with the same four fields, and a person-specific ruleset built around this file should use the same four so the two can be read side by side: **length**, **formality**, **shape**, and **what changes here** — the rules in §1–§16 that tighten, loosen, or reverse for this format. A format not listed takes the defaults above unchanged. §17 still governs: where a person's documented rules disagree with anything here, theirs win.

| Format | Length | Formality |
|---|---|---|
| Book chapter | Set by the argument | Formal |
| Article | Set by the reporting you actually have | Semi-formal to formal |
| Blog post | ≈400–1,200 words, one idea | Semi-formal |
| Social post (LinkedIn and similar) | Short — a screen, not a scroll | Semi-formal to informal |
| Short-video caption (TikTok, Instagram, Reels, Shorts) | One or two lines, hard ceiling | Informal |

### Book chapter

**Length.** However long the argument is. §1 still applies inside each section, so a long chapter is a sequence of tight sections, not one loose one.

**Formality.** Formal. Contractions thin out, fragments get rare, the joke gets drier. Formality is register, not decoration — the words in §4 read worse in a book, not better.

**Shape.** A chapter sits inside a book, so it can assume what earlier chapters established. Don't re-introduce the premise, don't preview the chapter to the reader ("In this chapter we'll look at..."), and don't close on a summary or a key-takeaways list. That bookend pair is §14.2 wearing a cover.

**What changes here.** Headings get sparser than a blog post's — not one every few hundred words. Bullets are the strongest tell in long-form prose: if material can be a paragraph, it's a paragraph. Quoted and cited material is held to §8 exactly; a chapter is where a rounded number does the most damage.

### Article

**Length.** What the reporting supports. Padding to hit a word count is §2's failure in a different costume.

**Formality.** Semi-formal to formal. Third person unless you're actually in the story.

**Shape.** The lead carries the finding — no scene-setting warm-up, no "in an era where" (§4). Headline in sentence case, and never a colon subtitle (§5.8).

**What changes here.** Attribution tightens: every claim traceable to something named, per §7. Not "experts say", not "studies show", not "reports suggest". Template section headings are this format's signature tell (§5.15) — a heading names what's actually in the section or it doesn't exist. And don't add a both-sides paragraph the reporting doesn't support (§14.4); balance is a finding, not a default.

### Blog post

**Length.** Roughly 400 to 1,200 words, carrying one idea. If it's carrying two, it's two posts.

**Formality.** Semi-formal. First person, contractions, and the occasional aside are all fine here.

**Shape.** The title states the claim, not the topic. Open on the claim. Stop when it's made — no "Conclusion" heading, no recap paragraph, no questions-and-answers section bolted on at the end, no "hope this helps".

**What changes here.** The search-engine furniture is out entirely: the opening that restates the search query back ("If you're looking for X, you're in the right place"), the "in this post we'll cover" contents list, the definitions section stalling in front of the point, and the numbered listicle whose number isn't real (§5.16).

### Social post (LinkedIn and similar)

**Length.** Short. One idea, and at most one ask — only if you actually want the answer (§3).

**Formality.** Semi-formal to informal, depending on the platform and the reader.

**Shape.** Line breaks do the work paragraphs would elsewhere. Front-load: the first line is all most readers will see.

**What changes here.** Nothing loosens. This platform's house style is more or less the tell list itself — one-line paragraphs stacked into an even ladder, mirrored contrasts ("Same X. Same Y. Different Z."), staccato negation runs, the significance close — and §5 bans all of it here exactly as everywhere else. That everyone else writes this way isn't an argument; it's why the pattern reads as machine-written. Vary the line lengths and let one line run long. No engagement bait ("Agree?" or "Thoughts?" as a reflex), and no hashtag wall — a few at the end, or none.

### Short-video caption (TikTok, Instagram, Reels, Shorts)

**Length.** One or two lines. Under about 150 characters, and a fragment is often the whole caption. **This is the hardest ceiling in this section, and it's the one rule here that matters more than the others.** People write short captions; machines write captions that explain. The length is the tell before a single word gets examined, so a caption that runs to a paragraph has already failed. If it feels incomplete, that's right — the video is carrying the content.

**Formality.** Informal. Lowercase is fine. No closing period is fine. A caption doesn't have to be a sentence.

**Shape.** The caption adds something the video doesn't have — a setup, an aside, a reaction, the one detail that makes the clip make sense. It never summarizes the video, never restates the on-screen text, and never narrates what's about to happen.

**What changes here.** Emoji are allowed, where §6 discourages them: one or two, inside the line, never as bullets or decoration. Hashtags go at the end, a few, not woven into the sentence. Cut the stacked closing asks — "follow for more", "link in bio", "watch till the end", "comment X below" — to at most one, and only when it's real. Don't explain the joke.
